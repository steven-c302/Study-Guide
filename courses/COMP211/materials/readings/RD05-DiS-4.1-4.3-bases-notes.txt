RD05 reading notes - Dive into Systems 4.1-4.3 (number bases, binary, hexadecimal) - study notes written for this guide
Unsigned means zero or positive only. Decimal is base 10: each digit is multiplied by a power of 10 by position (8425 = 8x10^3 + 4x10^2 + 2x10^1 + 5x10^0), and adding 1 to the digit 9 needs another digit because a digit cannot exceed base-1.
Binary is base 2: each digit (bit) is 0 or 1 and a position is worth a power of 2. Add 1 to a bit that is already 1 and it resets to 0 with a carry into the next bit. Labeling bits d0, d1, d2 ..., d0 is the rightmost (least significant). The prefix 0b marks binary; no prefix means decimal.
Binary suits hardware because a bit maps to two reliable electrical states. Decimal does not align with powers of two, so it is a poor way to reason about fixed-size binary data such as 16-bit addresses.
Hexadecimal is base 16: digits 0-9 then A-F for 10-15. Each hex digit is exactly 4 bits, so hex is a compact, human-readable notation for binary (this is why memory addresses are printed in hex even though they are stored as bits). Adding 1 to F gives 0 with a carry (0xF + 1 = 0x10). Prefix 0x.
Conversions: binary to decimal by summing powers of two; decimal to binary by subtracting the largest power of two that fits; binary to hex by grouping four bits from the right; hex to binary by expanding each digit to four bits.
